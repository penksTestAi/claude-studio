Great Circle Routes

Drag two airports on a flat map or a globe and see the shortest route between them bend, with its length and bearing checked.

Drag a dot, or focus it and use the arrow keys (Shift for degrees).

Why the route looks bent

A flat map has to stretch the sphere. On this one (equirectangular, longitude and latitude used as flat coordinates) a straight ruler line is not the shortest way. The shortest path on a sphere is an arc of a great circle: the circle you get by slicing the sphere through its centre. Drawn on the map, such an arc usually bulges toward the nearer pole: east-west distances shrink as you go poleward, so a detour toward the pole saves more than it costs. On the globe the same route is simply the tightest string you could pull between the two points.

The page measures the gap for the pair you chose: the dashed map-straight line is cut into short pieces, each piece's length is taken along the sphere and they are added up, then compared with the great circle. Winds, airspace and safety rules also shape real flight tracks; none of that is modelled here.

    How this differs from Tissot Circles

    Tissot Circles asks how a projection distorts: it puts circles of equal true size on Mercator and Equal Earth and measures how their shape and area change. This page asks the opposite question about one shortest path: given two points, where does the shortest route go, and how long is it, drawn on a plate carree map and a globe? Tissot Circles is about area and shape; this is about distance, bearing and the sphere-versus-ellipsoid gap. They share only the Natural Earth outline source.
