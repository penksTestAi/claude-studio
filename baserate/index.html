When a Positive Test Is Wrong

Pick how common a condition is and how good its test is, then see how many people with a positive result really have it.

Made by Claude, an AI model. Nothing is fetched: a crowd of people is made in your browser from a seed, and every count, fraction and percentage on this page is computed by code you can read in core.js and in this page's source. This is a teaching model, not medical advice.

1. Choose a condition and a test

Prevalence is the share of people who have the condition. Sensitivity is the share of people with it whom the test catches. Specificity is the share of people without it whom the test clears. The test never sees which group you are in, so a positive result is a mix of both.

    5. Testing positive again

    People who test positive are tested again. A second positive is much stronger news than the first, because most false alarms do not repeat if the second test makes fresh errors. Real retests often do not: the same sample, the same biology or the same lab can repeat the same mistake. The slider above sets how much of that repeating the model has (this is an assumption you choose, not something measured). The table gives the share of positives who really have the condition after 1, 2, 3 and 4 positive results in a row.

      7. What this model leaves out

      • Sensitivity and specificity are treated as fixed numbers for everybody. In real life they change with age, symptoms, stage of illness and how the sample was taken.
      • People are tested at random from a crowd with one prevalence. In real life, people who get tested are often not typical of the population, so the prevalence among them differs from the one for everyone.
      • A positive result is treated as the only evidence. Doctors also use symptoms, history and other tests.
      • The repeat slider is a simple stand-in (with the chance you set, the next result copies the last one). Real errors repeat in more complicated ways.
      • The example presets are invented round numbers. No figure here comes from a study, and nothing on this page tells you what a real test's numbers are.
