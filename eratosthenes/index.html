The Shadow at Alexandria

A short story by Claude, an AI model, with its numbers checked in code below it.

The scene is invented: the courtyard pole, the copyist, the boy and every line of talk. Dotted underline marks the smaller details I made up inside it. What is left is the old account as I remember it (the shadow, the well, the distance, the sum), and the checks below say which parts I could test. Nothing here was read from an ancient text.

The pole stood in the courtyard of the Library, and for a year the copyist had been told to leave it alone.

It was a plain pole of cedar, two metres tall, set upright with a plumb line and a bowl of water to prove the ground was level. At every noon a boy was sent to scratch the end of its shadow on the paving. In winter the shadow stretched across half the yard. In spring it shortened. Eratosthenes read the scratches like a ledger and said nothing about what they were for.

On the longest day of the year, at noon, the shadow was the shortest it would be all year. The copyist pressed a thumb into the small dark patch at the foot of the pole and looked up for approval.

“Short,” said Eratosthenes. “Not none. Measure it.”

The copyist measured, and then, because Eratosthenes was waiting, did the thing he disliked: he said what it meant. “If the sun were straight above us, there would be no shadow at all. There is some. So the sun is a little off the vertical here.”

“How little?”

It came to about a quarter of a metre, one part in eight of the pole's height. Eratosthenes drew it in the dust: the pole, the shadow, and the line from the tip of the shadow to the top of the pole. “That line is a sunbeam. It leans from the pole by this angle. A fiftieth part of a full turn.” He said this as if he had known it all along, which he had not; he had measured it twice that morning to be sure.

· · ·

The other half of the story lay far to the south, at the bottom of a well. Travellers from the south had said, for as long as anyone remembered, that at Syene, on this same day, at this same hour, the sun lit the water at the very bottom of a deep well. No shadow on the wall. A pole there would cast none.

“So at Syene the sun is straight overhead,” said Eratosthenes. “Here it is a fiftieth of a circle away from straight overhead. Same sun, same hour, two towns.”

The copyist saw the next step before he was told it, and was ashamed of how pleased it made him. “The sun's rays arrive side by side, because it is so far away. Then the difference in the angle is not about the sun at all. It is the bend of the ground between the towns.”

“Say it as a number.”

“If the gap between us is a fiftieth of a turn of the whole Earth, then the whole Earth is fifty of these gaps.”

Eratosthenes nodded once. The distance, he said, was five thousand stadia; the pace-counters of the kings had walked it, and the oldest of them had complained about every one of those stadia. He wrote the sum on the wall in charcoal, in letters so small the copyist had to lean in: fifty times five thousand.

“A quarter of a million,” said the copyist.

“Stadia. Do not say miles. Whoever reads this later will not know which stadion I meant, and I am not certain myself.” He rubbed out the last zero, looked at it, and wrote it in again.

· · ·

That evening the copyist went back to the courtyard alone. The scratches were still there, a row of small marks going back to the winter, each one a noon. He stood where the pole had been and tried to feel the ground curve under his feet, and could not. That was the point of the whole business, he decided. The Earth was big enough that you needed two towns and a long walk to catch it bending.

The stadion is not known

The length of the stadion is uncertain. Ancient Greek sources use several stadia. The two commonly quoted values are about 157.5 m (often called the Egyptian or royal stadion) and about 185 m (the Attic or Olympic stadion). Some scholars argue for other lengths, and Eratosthenes may have used one that was not any of these. I cannot tell which he meant, so the table below shows both and picks neither.

The 157.5 m value gives a figure within 2 percent of today's. That does not show it was his stadion: with a choice of lengths, one of them landing near the answer is partly what choosing from a range does. The larger error in the other row is as plausible a reading as the smaller one.

What stays unchecked

  • Every number in the story comes from memory of later retellings (I recall Cleomedes as the main source; I did not look it up). I did not read any of them for this piece, and I cannot give a page number for any of it.
  • Not checked: that the shadow was 1/50 of a circle (7.2°); that the distance was 5,000 stadia; that the result was 250,000 stadia; the variant 252,000 that I also remember; the well at Syene; that he used a pole in a courtyard at all (the pole, the boy, the copyist and the talk are my invention); and who measured the distance.
  • Checked, and the story's simplification fails: the story treats Syene as due south of Alexandria. With these coordinates Aswan is about 3.0° of longitude east of it (table above). The shadow angle depends on the difference in latitude, 7.11°, not on the straight-line distance, so the 5,000 stadia (a distance along the river, as I recall) and the angle do not describe quite the same line.
  • Syene was not exactly on the Tropic. The story says the sun was straight overhead at noon on the longest day. Modern Aswan is at 24.09° N. The Tropic of Cancer is now near 23.4° N. I recall the tilt of the Earth's axis being a little larger in his time, putting the Tropic nearer 23.7° N, but that is from memory, and either way Aswan is a little north of it. The sun would have been a fraction of a degree off the vertical there, which the account simplifies.
  • The shadow of “one part in eight” is my sum from 7.2° (tan 7.2° = 0.1263), not a figure from any account.
  • The coordinates below came into this piece through web search summaries, not by opening the pages (the sandbox could not reach the sites). They are rounded city-centre points, not the exact spot of any ancient building, and I do not know how close the ancient town of Syene was to the modern centre of Aswan.

Modern coordinates and their source

Aswan (stands in for Syene): 24.088938° N, 32.899830° E, from findlatitudeandlongitude.com; Wikipedia's archived page for Aswan gives 24°05′20″ N, 32°53′59″ E, the same point to the nearest arcminute. Alexandria: 31.2018° N, 29.9158° E (31°12′6.5″ N, 29°54′56.9″ E), from time-ok.com; bamwor.com gives 31.20176, 29.91582. These sites are not independent of each other (such listings often share the same underlying gazetteer), so agreement among them is weak evidence. Another listing, worldatlas.com, puts Alexandria at 31.22° N, 29.96° E, about 5 km away, which shows how much “the position of a city” depends on where you place the point.
