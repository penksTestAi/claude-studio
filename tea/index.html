Tea or Cha

Most languages' word for tea is a descendant of one Chinese word, but it left China by two roads and came out in two shapes: tea and cha. Here are 31 languages, one dot each, with its word.

Schematic, not to scale. On narrow screens only the selected language is labelled; the table lists all. The grey dots are a rough hand-made outline of the land, not borders and not survey data. Each marker sits near where the language is mainly spoken, not at a precise point; many languages cover far more ground.

1. One character, two sounds

The Chinese character 茶 is read chá in Mandarin and similarly in Cantonese. In Min Nan (Hokkien), spoken around the port of Xiamen, once called Amoy, on the coast of Fujian, it is usually given as tê. The two roads are usually explained by who heard which: people who got tea by ship from Fujian tended to hear the Min Nan form, and people who got it overland, or from Cantonese-speaking Macau, tended to hear a cha form. search snippets of Wikipedia's article on the etymology of tea suggest the sea/land story is a simplification (I could not open the article itself).

Same plant, same character, two local pronunciations. That is the whole story in one line; the rest is the detail.

2. The sea road, and the Dutch

The te family is usually linked to sea trade. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) began shipping tea to Europe in the early 1600s, and its Dutch word thee is the usual explanation for French thé, German Tee, and English tea. Whether the Dutch took the word straight from Min Nan or through Malay traders in Java is disputed. Spanish té, Swedish te, Malay teh and Sinhala tē are all on the list. Sinhala and Tamil are sometimes tied to the Dutch presence in Ceylon; I have not checked that link, and it is not needed to classify them.

Min Nan itself, listed here, is the source of the sea form.

3. The land road

The cha family is usually tied to overland trade across Central Asia: Persian chāy, Russian chay, Turkish çay, Arabic shāy (reported as a later borrowing from Persian), Hindi chāy. Chinese neighbours with long contact, Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese, also have cha-type words, and so do Mongolian and Tibetan. Swahili chai is cha-type too, and is generally thought to have arrived across the Indian Ocean from the north, though I did not trace it.

4. The Portuguese

Portuguese traders were based at Macau from the 1550s, among Cantonese speakers. Portuguese chá is cha-type even though it travelled by sea, and English cha (1590s) is reported to have come that way. So the rule is not that sea routes carry te; the rule of thumb is that the word follows the dialect of the port you buy from. English later picked up chai from Hindi too, so English has both.

5. The exceptions

Polish herbata is usually said to come from Latin herba thea, "tea herb". That makes it arguably a te-type word with a Latin front; I mark it "other" and that is a judgement call. Burmese laphet-yay is built from its own word for tea leaf, and I list it as "other" too, unverified.

Thai ชา (chaa) is cha-type, which may surprise anyone who expects the sea route to give te. I did not trace why, and I list it by what it sounds like, not by what geography predicts.

What this is based on, and what is not checked

  • Data basis: the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS), feature 138A "Tea", by Östen Dahl (2013), which sorts languages into words from Sinitic cha, words from te, and others. The idea, and the three-way sorting, come from there. wals.info/feature/138A
  • Second pass: the WALS site could not be opened here, only search snippets. They confirm the WALS totals (230 languages: 110 cha, 84 te, 36 other) and a few other datapoints (Afrikaans te; Basque, Tagalog and Punjabi cha; Lao other). None of these 31 was checked against its WALS value, and I cannot say they are all in the sample. Words, spellings and route sorting are from my own knowledge, not individually verified. Spellings in Sinhala, Tamil, Tibetan, Burmese, Hebrew, Arabic, Persian and Mongolian are the most likely to contain slips; a native reader should check them. Polish and Burmese are my own judgement calls.
  • The history (Dutch tea shipments from about 1606, Portuguese at Macau, Amoy as the source of te) is the commonly told account, partly echoed by search snippets of Wikipedia's "Etymology of tea", and I have kept it hedged. It is a simplification: scholars point to more complicated cases, and it is a story about words, not a precise record of trade.
  • The map outline is my own rough polygons (made on the fly into dots) and the marker positions are approximate, chosen by hand.
  • The 31 languages are a hand-picked list, not a sample. The counts above describe this list only, not the world.
